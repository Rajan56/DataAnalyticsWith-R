Did the heat recovery retrofit pay off?

A packaging plant fitted a heat recovery unit to one of its two board lines. Energy per tonne fell afterwards, and the project team reported the full drop as the saving. This analysis asks how much of it the retrofit truly caused, and what that means for the payback.

  • Welch t test
  • Difference in differences
  • Multiple regression
  • Robust standard errors
  • Model diagnostics
  • Logistic regression
  • Cross-validation
  • Interactive what-if model

The answer first

The retrofit works, but it saves about 29 % less than the before and after comparison suggests. The rest of the visible drop came from warmer weather, which lowered energy use on the untouched line as well.

Causal saving-37.7 kWh/t95 % CI -45.2 to -30.2
Worth per yearEUR 162 000range 130 000 to 195 000
Payback2.6 yearsnot 1.8, as first reported

Quality did not suffer, and about 180 tonnes of CO₂ are avoided each year. The recommendation is to extend the retrofit to line A, with the business case built on the corrected figure.

Step 1The business question

Line B received a heat recovery unit on its dryer section at the start of week 19. The investment was EUR 420 000. Management now has to decide whether to fit the same unit to line A. Three questions follow:

  1. How much did the retrofit reduce energy intensity, separated from season and product mix?
  2. Which process factors drive energy per tonne, and by how much?
  3. Did the retrofit affect product quality?
About the data. The company is fictional and the data are simulated in R with a fixed seed. A true retrofit effect of -38 kWh per tonne was built into the simulation, which makes it possible to check whether each method recovers the right answer. The methods are the ones I apply to real plant data.

Step 3The obvious test, and why it misleads

t_b <- t.test(energy_kwh_t ~ period, data = b)   # Welch test, unequal variances

Step 4Difference in differences

Line A serves as the control. The change on line B minus the change on line A removes everything the two lines share, such as season and energy mix. In a regression this is the interaction between line and period. Adding the process covariates removes day to day differences in what each line was producing.

The row lineB:periodAfter is the retrofit effect: -37.7 kWh per tonne (95 % CI -45.2 to -30.2, p < 0.001). That is very close to the true value of -38 built into the data. Without covariates the same design gives -24.3, which is less precise because the two lines happened to run a different product mix in the two periods.

Step 5What drives energy per tonne

The same model answers the second question. Read from the table above:

  • Throughput. Each extra tonne per hour lowers energy intensity by 13.6 kWh per tonne. Running the line full is an energy measure.
  • Moisture. Each percentage point of raw material moisture adds 11.8 kWh per tonne. Supplier specifications have an energy price.
  • Changeovers. Each changeover in a day adds 9.6 kWh per tonne, which gives scheduling a measurable energy value.
  • Grade. Heavy grade needs 33.6 kWh per tonne more than standard, so a fair energy KPI must be adjusted for mix.
  • Weather. Each degree of outdoor temperature lowers intensity by 2.2 kWh per tonne.

Together these explain 82 % of the daily variation.

Step 6Can the model be trusted?

A result that will carry an investment decision deserves checks. Four were run.

Parallel trends. Difference in differences assumes the lines moved together before the retrofit. A line by week interaction in the pre period tests this:

No evidence of diverging trends (p = 0.889).

Step 7Did quality suffer?

An energy saving that raises scrap is no saving. Quality deviation days on line B, before and after:

        Deviation
Period    0  1

The deviation rate is unchanged (chi-squared test, p > 0.99). A logistic regression across both lines confirms it while controlling for the process conditions:

logit_m <- glm(quality_dev ~ moisture_pct + changeovers + grade + throughput_tph + treated,
               data = d, family = binomial)

The retrofit has no detectable effect on quality (odds ratio 1.18, p = 0.661). What does raise the risk is moisture, changeovers and heavy grade. Under ten-fold cross-validation the model reaches an AUC of 0.67, against 0.73 in sample. That is useful for ranking risky days and too weak to automate a decision, which is worth saying plainly.

Step 8From coefficient to euros

Line B produces about 47 901 tonnes a year. At EUR 90 per MWh:

The corrected saving is EUR 162 000 a year, with a 95 % range of EUR 130 000 to 195 000, and about 180 tonnes of CO₂ avoided at an assumed 0.10 kg per kWh. Payback moves from 1.8 to 2.6 years. The investment still clears a normal hurdle, yet a business case for line A written on the naive figure would have overstated the benefit by EUR 67 000 a year.

Step 9Recommendations

  1. Approve the retrofit for line A on the corrected saving of about 37.7 kWh per tonne, and report the range, not a single number.
  2. Change the energy KPI to a weather and mix adjusted figure. The raw kWh per tonne rewards summer and punishes heavy grade.
  3. Treat scheduling and moisture as energy levers. Fewer changeovers and tighter incoming moisture cost little and are visible in the model.
  4. Use the quality model as a watch list for high-risk days, and collect more data before relying on it further.

NotesLimits and how to reproduce

The data are simulated, so real plant data would bring autocorrelation, sensor drift and unrecorded events that need their own treatment. With one treated line and one control, the design cannot separate the retrofit from any other change made to line B in the same week. Days are treated as independent observations.

To reproduce every number and figure on this page, download the two R scripts into one folder and run:

source("analysis.R")
